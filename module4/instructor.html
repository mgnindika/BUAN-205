Data visualization, with AI as your lab partner

In this module you will learn to turn a spreadsheet into a picture people understand in seconds. You'll build every chart in Excel, then ask AI to explain it, build it, and push it further. Your most important job is to check whether the AI got it right.

Five rules for working with AI in this class

  1. Upload the class file, or paste the data. If the AI can't open a file, copy the cells from Excel and paste them into the prompt.
  2. Never trust a number you didn't check. AI can miscount, drop rows, or make up values that sound right.
  3. Build it in Excel too. AI explains, while Excel proves. You need both skills for your career.
  4. Keep an AI log. Write down the prompts you used and one time the AI was wrong. You will hand this in with the project.
  5. Don't paste private data. Our class datasets are fine, but a future employer's customer list is not.

How to write a good prompt

A weak prompt says "make a chart." A strong prompt tells the AI five things. You don't need all five every time, but the more you give it, the better the answer you get back.

WhoTell it who the audience is: "Explain this to a restaurant owner with no stats background." DataGive it the data: attach the file, or paste the numbers. TaskName one clear job: "Build a crosstab of rating by price." FormatSay what shape the answer should take: "A table, then three bullet findings, under 150 words." CheckAsk it to check itself: "Show your totals so I can verify them."

Five moves to push any answer further

  • "Why did you pick that chart? What's the second-best choice?"
  • "Show me the numbers behind that claim."
  • "Give me the exact Excel steps to build this myself."
  • "What is wrong or misleading about your own chart?"
  • "What would a skeptical manager ask about this?"

3.1

Seeing before thinking

Preattentive attributes

Your brain notices some things before you even start thinking: color, size, shape, and length. These are called preattentive attributes. A good chart uses them to point the reader's eye where it should go. That lowers the cognitive load, which means the mental effort needed to understand the chart.

You just saw this with the 7s. The numbers didn't change. Only the color or size did, and counting became easy.

  • Color is very strong. But too many colors becomes noise, and about 1 in 12 men has some color blindness.
  • Size is weaker than it looks. People are bad at judging how much bigger one area is than another.
  • Length is the best tool for showing amounts. A longer bar means a bigger number, and our eyes judge length well.
Ask AI

Answer key: preattentive attributes (Levels 1–3)
Level 1

A strong answer says these are features the eye picks up in a fraction of a second, without effort: color, size, shape, and length. A good example is a monthly sales table where the one region that missed target is shown in red. The best attribute for amounts is length, because people compare lengths on a common baseline very accurately. That's why bar charts work.

Level 2

The managers under 15 are Williams (6) and Edwards (11). Jones has exactly 15, so he is not "fewer than 15." Watch for AI that includes Jones. Good designs:

  • a sorted bar chart with Williams and Edwards in a strong color and everyone else in light gray (length plus color);
  • a bar chart with a vertical reference line at 15 labeled "minimum" (length plus position).
Level 3

Replace color with intensity, meaning dark bars for the two and light gray for the rest, which survives black and white printing. Add bold labels or the reference line at 15. What gets worse is that the contrast is less striking than color, so the reader may need a short note such as "dark = under 15."

Think harder

Why is length better than area for showing quantity? Use a pizza example.

Answer key

A 16-inch pizza is only twice as wide as an 8-inch pizza, but it has four times the area. People looking at circles can't tell if one is 2 times or 4 times bigger. Bars only grow in one direction, so twice as long really looks twice as big. That's why bar charts beat bubble and pie charts when you need accuracy.

Data-ink ratio and decluttering scarf sales

Data-ink is ink that shows data. Non-data-ink is everything else: heavy gridlines, borders, shaded backgrounds, a legend for one line, and extra decimals. The data-ink ratio is data-ink divided by total ink. Push it up by removing what doesn't help. This is called decluttering. White space counts too, because empty space helps the data stand out.

Excel's default charts are cluttered. Always clean them before you show your boss.

Answer key

Only if you add data labels on the points that matter. Without an axis or labels, the reader has no idea whether a spike is 10 units or 10,000. Decluttering removes ink that carries no meaning, but the scale carries meaning.

Color that everyone can read

Color is the strongest attention-getter you have, which is exactly why it is so easy to waste. Two rules cover almost every business chart.

Rule one: gray is a color. Put everything in gray, then use one bright color for the single thing you want noticed. A chart where everything is colorful is a chart where nothing stands out. If your dashboard has a headline, the color should point at the evidence for that headline.

Rule two: never let color carry the meaning alone. About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women cannot reliably tell red from green. A red-and-green “bad vs good” chart is unreadable for two or three students in a class of thirty, and for a share of every audience you will ever present to.

What to do instead.

  • Use blue and orange rather than red and green. The contrast survives every common type of color blindness, and it still prints in grayscale.
  • Add a second signal. Label the bar, or order the categories, or use solid versus dashed lines. Then color becomes a bonus, not a requirement.
  • Four colors plus gray, maximum. Past that, readers stop matching the legend and give up.
  • Check it in gray. Screenshot your chart, desaturate it on your phone, and see whether you can still read it. If you cannot, neither can part of your audience.

A quick way to think about it: color for attention, position and length for value. People compare lengths accurately and colors badly, so never ask color to encode a number you want read precisely.

A demo that lands. Put a red/green chart on the projector and ask the class to find the worst performer. Then show the same chart desaturated. The room goes quiet, and the rule sticks for the rest of the semester.

Choosing the right chart

Every chart answers one question. Pick the question first, and the chart picks itself.

Most bad business charts are not ugly — they are the wrong shape for the question being asked. Before you touch Excel, finish this sentence out loud: “I want to show that …” The words you use point straight at the chart.

Four questions before you choose.

  1. What is my one sentence? If you cannot say it, no chart will say it for you.
  2. How many variables? One, two, or three? Three is the practical limit for one chart.
  3. Is one of them time? If yes, time goes on the horizontal axis, and a line is usually right.
  4. Are my categories ordered? Ordered (Jan–Dec, Good–Excellent) keeps its natural order. Unordered (branches, managers) gets sorted by value.

Two rules that settle most arguments. Time goes left to right, always — never sort months by size. And unordered categories get sorted, because an unsorted bar chart makes the reader do the ranking you were paid to do.

Tables and PivotTables

When a table beats a chart gossamer

Use a table when:

  • the reader needs exact numbers,
  • the reader needs to make precise comparisons, or
  • the values have different units or very different sizes, like dollars and head count.

Use a chart when the shape or the trend matters more than the exact number. Sometimes the best answer is both: a small chart with a table underneath.

Good table design follows three rules. Put lines only under headers and above totals. Left-align labels and right-align numbers. Keep the table plain.

Aggregation traps

A PivotTable makes it very easy to produce a number that is technically correct and completely misleading. Three traps cause most of the damage, and all three show up in student dashboards every semester.

1. The average of averages. If Branch A averages $300 over 100 sales and Branch B averages $400 over 900 sales, the combined average is not $350. It is close to $390, because B has nine times the weight. Averaging averages silently assumes every group is the same size. Always average the underlying rows, not the summary.

2. Comparing unequal periods. Three weeks of January against four weeks of February will always make February look better. Compare complete periods, or compare per-day figures. Our supermarket file ends on 30 March, so March is a short month in this data — a trap the Challenge rubric specifically checks for.

3. Simpson's paradox. The strangest one: a pattern that holds in every group can reverse when the groups are combined. It happens when group sizes differ and the grouping variable is related to both things you are measuring. This is not a curiosity — it is how honest people produce confident, wrong recommendations.

The habit that protects you. Every time you report an average or a percentage, ask two questions: “an average over how many rows?” and “would this still hold inside each group?” Put the row count on your dashboard next to any average. It costs one column and it is the single fastest way to catch this family of error.

Axis honesty and the dual-axis trap

The fastest way to lie with a chart is to leave the data alone and move the axis. You will be shown charts like this, and you will be tempted to make them.

Bars must start at zero. A bar says “this much” with its length, so if the axis starts at 140 instead of 0, a 5% difference can look like a 300% difference. This is not a style preference — it is the difference between a true chart and a false one.

Lines may start elsewhere, if you say so. A line says “this changed,” and it uses position rather than length, so a zoomed axis is legitimate when the change is genuinely small and genuinely matters. The rule is disclosure: label the axis clearly and never hide the break.

The dual-axis trap. A chart with one series on a left axis and another on a right axis lets you slide the two scales independently — which means you can make almost any two series look like they move together. The correlation is manufactured by your axis choices, not found in the data.

If you want to show that two things move together, use a scatter chart, where the relationship has to survive on its own. Keep dual axes for the rare case where the two series share a real, stated relationship, and label both axes loudly.

Small multiples

When you have many groups and one measure, the instinct is to put every group on one chart. With four series that works. With sixteen you get spaghetti: a tangle nobody can read, where the only visible information is that things are complicated.

Small multiples solve this by repeating one small chart once per group, all on the same axes, arranged in a grid. Because the shape of each panel is comparable, your eye finds the odd one out in seconds — which is exactly the job a regional manager needs done.

The one rule that matters: every panel must share the same scale. If Excel auto-scales each little chart to its own data, a store that moved 2% and a store that moved 20% will look identical, and the grid becomes worse than the spaghetti. Set the axis minimum and maximum by hand on the first panel, then copy the formatting to the rest.

Keep the panels small and strip them bare: no legend, no gridlines, one label per panel. The whole point is the shape, not the detail.

Small multiples and heat maps answer similar questions in different ways. A heat map is denser and better for spotting where something happened across two dimensions; small multiples keep the actual shape of each trend, so they are better when the pattern over time is what matters.

A parallel-coordinates plot gives each variable its own vertical axis. Each observation is a line that connects its values across the axes. A choropleth map colors each region by its value. A weather map of daily high temperatures is the classic example. In Excel you can build one with Insert, Maps, Filled Map.

3.6

Dashboards

A key performance indicator (KPI) is a number that tells you how the business is doing right now, such as sales today, calls waiting, or orders late. A data dashboard puts several KPIs on one screen and updates as new data arrives.

Good dashboards follow these rules:

  • All KPIs for one part of the business fit on one screen, with no scrolling.
  • Use several dashboards if needed, rather than one giant one.
  • Every KPI should connect to a decision someone makes.
  • Everything from 3.1 still applies, including a high data-ink ratio and smart use of preattentive attributes.
  • Use color to call attention to something, not to decorate.

Example: Grogan Oil runs an IT call center in Austin. Its dashboard shows five things:

  • call volume by problem type each hour (stacked column),
  • the share of employee time spent on each problem type (column),
  • unresolved cases waiting over 15 minutes (bar),
  • call volume by office (bar), and
  • how long cases take to resolve (histogram).

Track B: bring your own real data

Everything above is Track A: one clean file, one manager, one question. It is the right way to learn the moves. Track B is the same competition run on data nobody cleaned for you — which is the only kind you will meet after graduation. Your team picks one track.

What changes, and what does not. The rubric, the three-minute pitch, the one-screen dashboard, the AI log, and every rule about building charts in Excel are identical. Two things are added: you choose the question, and you have to defend the data itself.

Step 1: pick a dataset that can actually carry a dashboard

Most failed projects fail here, in week one, by picking something too small or too flat. Before you commit, check all four:

  1. At least 200 rows. Fewer, and every chart is a handful of points.
  2. At least one category column you can group by — region, product, type, department.
  3. At least two number columns, so you can show a relationship and not just a ranking.
  4. A time or place column. Without one, you cannot show a trend or draw a map, and you lose half the toolkit.

Then say your question out loud in one sentence, with a decision attached: “Which <category> should <someone> <do something about> next <period>?” If you cannot finish that sentence, the dataset is not ready.

Step 3: the data audit (this is graded)

Real data is dirty, and pretending otherwise is the fastest way to lose points. Before you chart anything, run this audit and keep the answers — one slide of it goes in your hand-in.

  1. How many rows and columns? Write the number down. Check it again after every filter.
  2. What is missing? Count blanks per column. Decide what to do and say so: drop them, leave them out of that one chart, or show them as their own category.
  3. Any duplicates? Check whether the same record appears twice.
  4. Does every column mean what its name says? Find the source's data dictionary. Columns named value or amount hide more than they tell.
  5. What are the units? Dollars or thousands of dollars? Percent or decimal? Get this wrong and every number on your dashboard is wrong.
  6. What period does it cover, and is the last one complete? A partial final month makes any trend look like a collapse.
  7. Any impossible values? Negative ages, future dates, a 400% share. Sort each column both ways and look at the ends.
  8. Who collected it, and who was left out? Every dataset counts some people and misses others. That gap is your honest limitation.

Answer key: running Track B

Approve datasets in person, and be strict. Two minutes per team, with the file open. The four checks catch almost everything: under 200 rows, no category column, only one numeric column, or no time or place. Teams argue hardest for datasets they find interesting but that fail check 1 — hold the line, because a thin dataset produces a thin dashboard and there is no rescuing it in week two.

The three failure modes, in order of how often they happen.

  1. Scope. The team picks a topic, not a question. Fix by making them finish the sentence “Which X should Y do something about next Z?” out loud before they leave the room.
  2. Cleaning swallows the project. A team spends both weeks reshaping and arrives with two charts. Cap it: if the data is not chart-ready by the end of week one, they move to Track A. Say this when you announce the tracks, not when it happens.
  3. Units. Thousands read as ones, decimals read as percents. This is the one that survives all the way to the pitch, because the charts look perfectly reasonable. Ask every Track B team “what are the units of this axis?” during judging.

Good picks from past experience. City 311 or permit data (clear categories, real dates, obvious decisions), BLS employment by metro, FRED series paired with a company's own numbers, Census income by county — which also sets up the aggregation trap from 3.2 if they average county medians.

Picks to steer away from. Anything with one row per country per year and nothing else; social media scrapes with no documentation; Kaggle sets with no data dictionary, where nobody can say what the columns mean; and any topic where the team already knows the answer they want, because they will build the dashboard to prove it.

Why Best Data Detective matters. Without it, Track B punishes teams for picking messy data, and everybody quietly chooses the cleanest file they can find. The extra award pays teams for honesty, which is the behaviour the whole module is trying to build. Announce it at the same time as the tracks.

If you run both tracks in one class, judge them together with the same rubric but read the Accuracy line differently: Track A accuracy means the numbers match the workbook, Track B accuracy means that plus defensible, disclosed cleaning. Say this to the judges before they score.

Enter at Excellent, $30–39, where waits are the longest at 34 minutes, with 28 competitors. The risk is that delivering Excellent quality is costly and the rating isn't guaranteed. Also accept Very Good at $30–39 or $40–49 if it's argued with evidence. What matters is that the reason and the risk come from the data.

AI mistakes to watch for in the logs
  • Price bins that overlap (10–20, 20–30).
  • Ratings in alphabetical order (Excellent, Good, Very Good).
  • Totals that don't add to 300.
  • Treating the 0.5-minute average (2 restaurants) as a real finding.
  • Claiming that price causes wait time.

Give full AI-log points only when the error described is specific and the team shows how they caught it.
