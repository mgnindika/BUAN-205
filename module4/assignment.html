1. The situation

You are an analytics team. A manager has one question and no patience for a chart that needs explaining. Your dashboard has to answer that question on one screen, and every number on it has to be defensible when someone asks "where did that come from?"

That is the whole assignment. The two tracks below differ only in whose data you use and who writes the question.

2. Choose your track

Your team picks one track and tells me which at the start of the build week. Both are graded on the same 100-point rubric. Neither is "the easy one" — Track A asks you to find something non-obvious in data everyone else also has; Track B asks you to make messy data trustworthy.

Track A The supermarket brief

You are the analytics team for a supermarket chain with three branches. The regional manager asks:

“Where should we focus next quarter to grow sales?”

Answer her with one screen of charts built in Excel from SuperMarket_Analysis.xlsx — 1,000 purchases across three branches, January to March 2019.

Where to start

  • Practice sets A–C in the notes are built on this exact file. They are your toolkit, not extra homework.
  • You may add helper columns — Hour, Day of week, Month — from the existing columns.
  • Watch the calendar: the data stops on 30 March, so March is a short month. Comparing raw monthly totals will make March look like a collapse when it isn't. The rubric checks for this.

Track B Bring your own real data

Same assignment, run on data nobody cleaned for you — which is the only kind you will meet after graduation. You choose the question, and you have to defend the data itself.

Step 1 — Pick a dataset that can carry a dashboard

Most failed projects fail here, in week one, by picking something too small or too flat. Before you commit, check all four:

  • At least 200 rows. Fewer, and every chart is a handful of points.
  • At least one category column to group by — region, product, type, department.
  • At least two number columns, so you can show a relationship and not just a ranking.
  • A time or place column. Without one you cannot show a trend or draw a map, and you lose half the toolkit.

Then finish this sentence out loud: “Which <category> should <someone> <do something about> next <period>?” If you cannot finish it, the dataset is not ready.

Get your dataset approved before you build anything. Bring the file and your one-sentence question. Approval takes about two minutes and saves a wasted week. A team whose data is still not chart-ready at the end of week one moves to Track A.

Step 3 — Check your data before you chart it

Real data is dirty, and pretending otherwise is the fastest way to lose points under Accuracy. You do not hand this in, but work through it before you build:

  1. How many rows and columns? Write it down. Check again after every filter.
  2. What is missing? Count blanks per column, and decide what to do about them.
  3. Any duplicates? Does the same record appear twice?
  4. Does every column mean what its name says? Find the data dictionary. Columns named value or amount hide more than they tell.
  5. What are the units? Dollars or thousands? Percent or decimal? Get this wrong and every number on your dashboard is wrong.
  6. What period does it cover, and is the last one complete? A partial final month makes any trend look like a collapse.
  7. Any impossible values? Negative ages, future dates, a 400% share. Sort each column both ways and look at the ends.
  8. Who collected it, and who was left out? That gap is your honest limitation.

Finding a flaw is worth more than having clean data. A team that finds a real problem, handles it sensibly and says so on the dashboard scores higher than a team whose data happened to be tidy.

One warning. Do not pick a topic too big to answer on one screen. “Climate change” is not a project. “Which three New Orleans ZIP codes waited longest for permit approval last year?” is.

3. Rules

These apply to both tracks.

  1. Build every chart in Excel. AI can suggest, explain, write formulas and critique, but it cannot draw your charts for you.
  2. Every number must be traceable. It comes from a PivotTable or a formula in your workbook, on a sheet behind the dashboard.
  3. One screen at 100% zoom, with no scrolling.
  4. Put your recommendation on the dashboard, as a one-sentence headline at the top.
  5. No 3-D charts. No pie chart with more than three slices.
  6. Four colors plus gray, maximum. Use color for attention, gray for context.
  7. Bars start at zero. Lines may not, if the axis is clearly labelled.
  8. State one honest limitation of the data on the dashboard.
  9. Track B only: add a source line — who published the data, what it covers, and when you downloaded it.

7. Using AI on this assignment

AI use is expected, not merely allowed. The skill being graded is not whether you used it — it is whether you checked it.

Encouraged: asking AI to explain a concept, suggest chart types, write an Excel formula, draft a headline, critique your dashboard, or play a skeptical manager.

Not allowed: having AI generate the charts or the dashboard itself, or handing in numbers you have not verified against your own PivotTables.

AI is confidently wrong often enough that Accuracy is 25 points. Check every figure it gives you against the workbook before it reaches the dashboard.
